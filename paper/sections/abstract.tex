We show that a 9B open-weight model, adapted for \$20 of GPU time, answers
closed-book factual questions about a real hardware platform more accurately
than Claude Opus~5 (92.8\% vs.\ 72.0\% on a 125-question layered audit set;
81.6\% vs.\ 72.2\% on the full 1{,}776-question bank). The setting is the one
every chip company faces: the knowledge an internal assistant needs---register
maps, memory maps, build pins, driver conventions, issue history---appears in
no pretraining corpus, and the obvious recipe, continued pretraining on the raw
internal corpus, \emph{silently fails}: training loss drops by more than half
while question-answering recall of memorized facts does not move at all. This
report documents the decision chain of a small, fully open study on the PULP
Carfield/Cheshire platform: which testbed to use (and why a popular open-source
chip cannot serve as one), continued pretraining versus fine-tuning versus
retrieval, and---centrally---a knowledge-rewriting augmentation stage whose
design rules (full coverage of all facts, template diversity over repetition,
reversed statements, whole-table narratives against similar-fact interference)
each trace to a measured failure of a simpler variant. We also report how our
own auto-generated benchmark misled us twice (lexically leaky multiple-choice
questions, format-incomplete few-shot exemplars) and how we repaired it. All
code, data pipeline, benchmark, and model weights are released; the entire
study, including every ablation and baseline, cost about \$330.
